\subsection{Linear combinations and span}

A linear combination is one result of choosing vectors and coefficients.
The span collects every result obtainable when those coefficients and the
finite selection of vectors are allowed to vary.

\begin{definition}
  A linear combination of \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\in V\) is a vector of the form
  \[
    a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n,\qquad a_1,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb F.
  \]
  For \(S\subseteq V\), its span, denoted \(\operatorname{span}(S)\), is the
  set of all finite linear combinations of elements of \(S\). Explicitly,
  \[
    \operatorname{span}(S)=
      \left\{\sum_{j=1}^n a_jv_j\;\middle|\;
        \begin{gathered}
          n\ge0,\quad a_1,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb F,\\
          v_1,\ldots,v_n\in S
        \end{gathered}
      \right\},
  \]
  where \(n\) ranges over the nonnegative integers. The subset \(S\)
  need not be finite, but each linear combination uses finitely many
  of its vectors.
\end{definition}

The empty linear combination is the zero vector, so
\(\operatorname{span}(\varnothing)=\{0\}\). A vector space is
\emph{finite-dimensional} when it has a finite spanning set. Unless explicitly
stated otherwise, the vector spaces in this book are finite-dimensional.

\subsection{Linear independence}

\begin{definition}
  A subset \(S\subseteq V\) is linearly independent if, for every finite
  selection of distinct vectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\in S\),
  \[
    a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n=0
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    a_1=\cdots=a_n=0.
  \]
  The empty set is linearly independent.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, coefficients in a linear combination of distinct vectors from
an independent set are unique. If
\[
  a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n=b_1v_1+\cdots+b_nv_n,
\]
then \(a_j=b_j\) for each \(j\).
Thus independence answers a different question from spanning. Spanning asks
whether a vector can be constructed from the chosen vectors. Independence
asks whether two different coefficient choices can construct the same vector.

\begin{definition}
  A subset is linearly dependent if some finite selection of distinct
  vectors from it satisfies
  \[
    a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n=0
  \]
  with at least one coefficient nonzero.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Bases and dimension}

\begin{definition}
  A basis of \(V\) is a linearly independent subset that spans \(V\).
  The dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space is the number of
  vectors in a basis.
\end{definition}

Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis, as
\autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-extension} will show. The general
existence result is proved in \autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-existence}.
The zero vector space has the empty basis. The three standard coordinate
vectors span \(\R^3\) and are independent, so \(\dim\R^3=3\).

More generally, a basis \(\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}\) of \(\R^n\) allows
every vector in \(\R^n\) to be written uniquely as
\(a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n\). The spanning property guarantees that the
representation exists, and linear independence makes its coefficients unique.

An ordered basis makes these coefficients into coordinates. For example,
with an ordered basis \(E=(e_1,e_2)\) of a real two-dimensional space,
\[
  v=xe_1+ye_2
\]
identifies \(v\) by the coordinate column \((x,y)^T\).
\autoref{fig:linear-algebra-i:basis-coordinate-grid} shows how repeated
steps in the two basis directions build a coordinate grid. The lines need
not meet at right angles. Uniqueness of the coefficients, rather than the
angles in the drawing, makes this a coordinate system.

\begin{theorem}[Comparison of independent and spanning sets]
  \label{thm:linear-algebra-i:comparison-independent-spanning}
  Let \(T\) be a finite spanning set of a vector space \(V\). Every
  linearly independent subset \(S\subseteq V\) is finite, and
  \[
    |S|\leq|T|.
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{pendingreview}
\begin{proof}
  Write \(n=|T|\). If \(n=0\), then \(V=\{0\}\), whose only independent
  subset is empty. Suppose \(n>0\) and that \(S\) contains distinct vectors
  \(u_1,\ldots,u_{n+1}\). We will replace the vectors of \(T\), one at a
  time, by \(u_1,\ldots,u_n\), preserving the spanning property.

  At step \(j\), write the current spanning list as
  \[
    u_1,\ldots,u_{j-1},w_j,\ldots,w_n.
  \]
  Its first \(j-1\) entries are the vectors already chosen from \(S\).
  Since the list spans \(V\), there are coefficients such that
  \[
    u_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}a_i u_i+\sum_{i=j}^n b_iw_i.
  \]
  At least one \(b_i\) is nonzero. Otherwise \(u_j\) would be a linear
  combination of \(u_1,\ldots,u_{j-1}\), contradicting the independence
  of the chosen vectors from \(S\). For an index \(\ell\) with
  \(b_\ell\ne0\), solve this equation for \(w_\ell\). It expresses
  \(w_\ell\) as a linear combination of \(u_j\) and the other vectors
  in the current list. Replacing \(w_\ell\) by \(u_j\) therefore leaves
  a spanning list. Reorder the remaining \(w_i\) to prepare the next step.

  After \(n\) steps, \(u_1,\ldots,u_n\) span \(V\). In particular,
  \(u_{n+1}\) is a linear combination of them, again contradicting
  independence. Thus \(S\) cannot contain \(n+1\) distinct vectors.
  It is consequently finite and has at most \(n=|T|\) elements.
\end{proof}
\end{pendingreview}

% These finite-dimensional consequences supply the basis-extension
% prerequisite used later in the book. Lucas authorized their statements
% and proofs in the November 10, 2026 proof-completion request.
\begin{pendingreview}
\begin{proposition}[Finite basis extension]
  \label{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-extension}
  Every linearly independent subset of a finite-dimensional vector space
  \(V\) is contained in a finite basis of \(V\). In particular, \(V\)
  has a finite basis.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Let \(S\subseteq V\) be independent, and choose a finite spanning set
  \(T=\{t_1,\ldots,t_n\}\) of \(V\). By
  \autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:comparison-independent-spanning}, \(S\)
  is finite. Starting with \(S\), consider \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\) in order.
  Add \(t_j\) to the current set precisely when it is outside its span.

  Each addition preserves independence. Indeed, if the current independent
  set is \(\{v_1,\ldots,v_q\}\) and
  \[
    a_1v_1+\cdots+a_qv_q+bt_j=0,
  \]
  a nonzero \(b\) would express \(t_j\) as a linear combination of the
  current vectors. Since \(t_j\) was outside their span, \(b=0\), and
  independence then gives \(a_1=\cdots=a_q=0\).

  The resulting set is finite, independent, and contains \(S\). Every
  element of \(T\) belongs to its span, whether it was added or was already
  in the span when considered. The resulting set therefore spans \(V\)
  and is a basis. Taking \(S=\varnothing\) gives the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The size of a finite basis]
  \label{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-cardinality}
  Any two bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same finite
  number of elements. Thus its dimension is well-defined.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
  Any basis is finite by
  \autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:comparison-independent-spanning}, applied
  to a finite spanning set of the space. If \(E\) and \(F\) are two bases,
  the same theorem gives \(|E|\leq|F|\), since \(E\) is independent and
  \(F\) spans. Interchanging \(E\) and \(F\) gives \(|F|\leq|E|\).
  Hence \(|E|=|F|\).
\end{proof}
\end{pendingreview}

\subsection{Subspaces}

\begin{definition}
  A nonempty subset \(T\subseteq V\) is a subspace if it is closed under
  linear combinations. That is, for \(v,w\in T\) and \(a,b\in\mathbb F\),
  \[
    av+bw\in T.
  \]
  A subspace inherits addition and scalar multiplication from \(V\).
\end{definition}

Equivalently, every finite linear combination of elements of \(T\) lies in
\(T\). Every subspace contains the zero vector. Choose \(v,w\in T\),
which is possible because \(T\) is nonempty. Taking \(a=b=0\) in the
closure condition gives \(0v+0w=0_V\in T\).

\begin{pendingreview}
\begin{proposition}[Subspaces of a finite-dimensional space]
  \label{thm:linear-algebra-i:finite-dimensional-subspaces}
  If \(T\) is a subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space \(V\), then
  \(T\) is finite-dimensional, and every basis of \(T\) extends to a basis
  of \(V\). Moreover,
  \[
    \dim T\leq\dim V,
    \qquad
    \dim T=\dim V\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T=V.
  \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Put \(n=\dim V\). Every independent subset of \(T\) is independent
  in \(V\), so its size is at most \(n\) by
  \autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:comparison-independent-spanning}.
  The possible sizes of independent subsets of \(T\) form a nonempty
  subset of \(\{0,\ldots,n\}\), since the empty set is independent.
  Choose an independent subset \(B\subseteq T\) of largest possible size.

  If \(B\) did not span \(T\), there would be a vector \(t\in T\)
  outside its span. Adjoining \(t\) would preserve independence, by the
  coefficient argument in the proof of
  \autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-extension}. This contradicts the
  choice of \(B\). Thus \(B\) is a finite basis of \(T\).

  Every basis of \(T\) is an independent subset of \(V\), so
  \autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-extension} extends it to a basis of
  \(V\). Consequently \(\dim T\leq\dim V\). If these dimensions are
  equal, the extended basis has no additional vectors. The original basis
  of \(T\) then spans \(V\), giving \(T=V\). Conversely, \(T=V\)
  immediately gives equality of their dimensions.
\end{proof}
\end{pendingreview}

\subsection{Zorn's lemma and infinite-dimensional bases}
\label{sec:linear-algebra-i:zorns-lemma}

In infinite-dimensional spaces, a basis \(S\) is still linearly independent, and
each vector is a \emph{finite} linear combination of elements of \(S\).

% The supplied notes invoke Zorn's lemma for basis existence. Lucas
% authorized the full argument on November 10, 2026. Zorn's lemma is an
% explicit set-theoretic prerequisite, not a consequence of the finite theory.
\begin{pendingreview}
The finite basis-extension argument examines a finite spanning set one vector
at a time. For a space with no finite spanning set, we instead use a
set-theoretic principle that guarantees a maximal independent set.

Let \(\mathcal F\) be a nonempty family of sets, ordered by inclusion.
A \emph{chain} in \(\mathcal F\) is a subfamily \(\mathcal C\) such
that any two of its members are comparable by inclusion. An \emph{upper
bound} for this chain is a member \(U\in\mathcal F\) containing every
member of \(\mathcal C\). A member \(M\in\mathcal F\) is
\emph{maximal} if no member of \(\mathcal F\) properly contains \(M\).
Maximality does not require \(M\) to contain every other member.

\textbf{Zorn's lemma \cite{Zorn1935TransfiniteAlgebra}.}
If every chain in a nonempty family \(\mathcal F\) has an upper bound
in \(\mathcal F\), then \(\mathcal F\) has a maximal member.

We use this inclusion-ordered form of Zorn's lemma as a set-theoretic
prerequisite. It is equivalent to the axiom of choice. The finite-dimensional
arguments above do not require this principle. In the basis-existence proof,
the sets are independent subsets of \(V\), and the upper bound for a chain
will be its union. Maximality then forces the resulting independent set to
span \(V\).

\begin{theorem}[Existence of a basis]
  \label{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-existence}
  Every vector space has a basis.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let \(\mathcal I\) be the family of all linearly independent subsets of
  \(V\), ordered by inclusion. This family is nonempty because
  \(\varnothing\in\mathcal I\). We first show that every chain in
  \(\mathcal I\) has an upper bound in \(\mathcal I\).

  For a nonempty chain \(\mathcal C\), let
  \(U=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal C}A\). Every finite selection of vectors
  from \(U\) lies in a single member of \(\mathcal C\). Indeed, choose
  a chain member containing each vector and take the largest of these
  finitely many members, using their comparability. Since that member is
  independent, any zero linear combination of the selected vectors has
  all coefficients zero. Thus \(U\) is independent and is an upper bound
  for \(\mathcal C\). The empty chain has the independent upper bound
  \(\varnothing\).

  Zorn's lemma now gives an inclusion-maximal independent subset
  \(B\subseteq V\). Suppose \(v\in V\) were outside
  \(\operatorname{span}(B)\). In any relation
  \[
    av+b_1e_1+\cdots+b_qe_q=0,
    \qquad e_1,\ldots,e_q\in B
  \]
  with the \(e_i\) distinct, a nonzero \(a\) would express \(v\) as a
  finite linear combination of vectors from \(B\). Hence \(a=0\), and
  independence of \(B\) gives \(b_1=\cdots=b_q=0\). Therefore
  \(B\cup\{v\}\) would be independent, contradicting maximality.
  It follows that \(B\) spans \(V\) and is a basis.
\end{proof}
\end{pendingreview}

\begin{example}
  The vector space \(\mathcal P\) of polynomials in \(x\) with real
  coefficients has the basis
  \[
    \{1,x,x^2,\ldots\}.
  \]
  \begin{pendingreview}
  Every polynomial is a finite linear combination of these monomials.
  Such a combination is the zero polynomial only when all its coefficients
  are zero, so the monomials are independent as well as spanning.
  \end{pendingreview}
\end{example}

For the vector space \(C(\R)\) of continuous functions \(\R\to\R\),
\autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:basis-existence} guarantees a basis. No explicit
basis is constructed here. This example motivates the study of topological
vector spaces.

\subsection{From bases to linear transformations}
\label{sec:linear-algebra-i:bases-to-transformations}

% Source: Lucas's conceptual notes, images/primitives/IMG_9444--9447.jpeg.
The same linear combinations connect vector spaces to maps between them.
A \emph{linear map}, also called a \emph{linear transformation}, is a map
\(L:V\to W\) between vector spaces over the same field that satisfies,
for every \(a,b\in\mathbb F\) and \(v,w\in V\),
\[
  L(av+bw)=aL(v)+bL(w).
\]
It preserves the way a vector is assembled. If \(v=ae_1+be_2\), then
\[
  L(v)=aL(e_1)+bL(e_2).
\]
The coefficients \(a,b\) are retained, while the building blocks
\(e_1,e_2\) are replaced by their images. Since a basis describes every
input uniquely, specifying its images determines a linear map everywhere.

In the real plane, consider the shear \(S(x,y)=(x+y,y)\), illustrated in
\autoref{fig:linear-algebra-i:shear-grid-overview}. Its action on two
basis vectors determines the action on the entire grid. The origin stays
fixed, and repeated equal steps in a basis direction become repeated equal
steps in its image direction. This is the geometric content of preserving
linear combinations.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/linear-map-grid-overview}
  \caption[A grid under a shear]{Schematic of \(S(x,y)=(x+y,y)\).
    The square grid becomes a parallelogram grid. Within each family,
    lines remain parallel and equally spaced, although angles change.
    The two arrows show the images of the standard basis vectors.}
  \label{fig:linear-algebra-i:shear-grid-overview}
\end{figure}

To calculate such an action, we choose output coordinates and record the
coordinates of the basis-vector images as columns of a matrix.
Matrix multiplication then forms their linear
combination using the input coefficients. This is the link between the
basis language of this chapter and the column calculations in
\autoref{sec:linear-algebra-i:matrix-multiplication}.
The general definition of linearity and its grid interpretation are developed
in \autoref{sec:linear-algebra-i:linear-functions-and-maps}, and
\autoref{sec:linear-algebra-i:change-of-basis-for-functions-and-maps}
explains how the matrix records a map in chosen input and output bases.
