\subsection{Matrices in standard bases}

Let \(L:\mathbb F^m\to\mathbb F^n\) be linear, and let
\(e_1,\ldots,e_m\) be the standard basis of \(\mathbb F^m\).
Write \(A_j=L(e_j)\), and form the matrix \(A\) whose columns are
\(A_1,\ldots,A_m\).
% September 17 PDF p. 23, expanded at Lucas's request on November 11.
\begin{pendingreview}
For \(v=v^1e_1+\cdots+v^me_m\), linearity gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    L(v)&=L(v^1e_1+\cdots+v^me_m)\\
      &=v^1L(e_1)+\cdots+v^mL(e_m)\\
      &=v^1A_1+\cdots+v^mA_m\\
      &=\begin{bmatrix}A_1&\cdots&A_m\end{bmatrix}
        \begin{bmatrix}v^1\\\vdots\\v^m\end{bmatrix}=Av.
  \end{aligned}
\]
The last equality is precisely the column definition of matrix multiplication.
Each input coordinate multiplies the image of its corresponding basis vector.
\end{pendingreview}
Thus a linear map is represented by the matrix of its values on the
standard basis. Conversely, an \(n\)-by-\(m\) matrix \(A\) defines the
linear map \(v\mapsto Av\).

\begin{example}[A map determined by two basis-vector images]
  \label{ex:linear-algebra-i:two-basis-images-matrix}
  % Source: September 17 PDF p. 22.
  \begin{pendingreview}
  If the linear map \(L:\mathbb F^2\to\mathbb F^3\) satisfies
  \(L(e_1)=a=(a^1,a^2,a^3)^T\) and
  \(L(e_2)=b=(b^1,b^2,b^3)^T\), then
  every input has the form \(v=v^1e_1+v^2e_2\), and
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      L(v)&=L(v^1e_1+v^2e_2)\\
        &=v^1L(e_1)+v^2L(e_2)\\
        &=v^1\begin{bmatrix}a^1\\a^2\\a^3\end{bmatrix}
          +v^2\begin{bmatrix}b^1\\b^2\\b^3\end{bmatrix}\\
        &=\begin{bmatrix}a^1&b^1\\a^2&b^2\\a^3&b^3\end{bmatrix}
          \begin{bmatrix}v^1\\v^2\end{bmatrix}.
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  There are two columns because the input has two basis vectors, and three
  rows because each image has three coordinates. No further values of
  \(L\) need to be specified. Its values on \(e_1,e_2\) already determine
  its value on every linear combination of them.
  \end{pendingreview}
\end{example}

The correspondence \(L\mapsto A\) is a bijective linear map
\[
  \mathcal L(\mathbb F^m,\mathbb F^n)
    \longrightarrow \mathrm M_{n\times m}(\mathbb F).
\]
\begin{pendingreview}
It is one-to-one because equal matrices give equal basis-vector images and
hence equal values at every input. Every matrix occurs, since multiplication
by that matrix defines a linear map. Addition and scalar multiplication of
maps act on each basis-vector image separately, so the correspondence also
preserves these operations.
\end{pendingreview}

\subsection{Matrices in arbitrary bases}

The standard-basis formula combines three ingredients already encountered.
An input basis gives coefficients, the map determines the images of its
basis vectors, and an output basis supplies coordinates for those images.
The same construction works for abstract finite-dimensional vector spaces.

Choose ordered bases \(E=[e_1,\ldots,e_m]\) of \(V\) and
\(F=[f_1,\ldots,f_n]\) of \(W\), and let \(L:V\to W\) be linear.
The basis \(E\) assigns each vector in \(V\) a unique coordinate column
in \(\mathbb F^m\), and \(F\) does the same for \(W\) in
\(\mathbb F^n\). These coordinate descriptions allow the vectors
themselves to be functions, polynomials, or other vector-space objects.
No containment of \(V\) or \(W\) in a Euclidean space is assumed.

% Sources: September 17 PDF pp. 26--29. Lucas requested this full
% first-principles derivation on November 11, 2026.
\begin{pendingreview}
The row notation for a basis represents a linear combination. For scalar
columns \(a\in\mathbb F^m\) and \(b\in\mathbb F^n\), it means
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    Ea&=e_1a^1+\cdots+e_ma^m,\\
    Fb&=f_1b^1+\cdots+f_nb^n.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Thus \(Ea\) is a vector in \(V\), and \(Fb\) is a vector in \(W\).
The basis vectors themselves may be functions or polynomials.

Since \(F\) is a basis of \(W\), each image \(L(e_j)\) has a unique
expansion in its vectors. Writing these expansions separately gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    L(e_1)&=f_1M^1_1+\cdots+f_nM^n_1,\\
      &\ \vdots\\
    L(e_m)&=f_1M^1_m+\cdots+f_nM^n_m.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Place the coefficients of \(L(e_j)\) in column \(j\) of a matrix,
\[
  M=\begin{bmatrix}
    M^1_1&\cdots&M^1_m\\
    \vdots&&\vdots\\
    M^n_1&\cdots&M^n_m
  \end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb F^{n\times m}.
\]
Column \(j\) belongs to input basis vector \(e_j\), and row \(i\)
records its image's coefficient in output direction \(f_i\).
The \(j\)-th column \(M_j\) therefore satisfies
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    FM_j&=\begin{bmatrix}f_1&\cdots&f_n\end{bmatrix}
      \begin{bmatrix}M^1_j\\\vdots\\M^n_j\end{bmatrix}\\
      &=f_1M^1_j+\cdots+f_nM^n_j=L(e_j).
  \end{aligned}
\]
This equality holds for every \(j\). By column-by-column multiplication,
\(FM\) is consequently the row of vectors
\(\begin{bmatrix}L(e_1)&\cdots&L(e_m)\end{bmatrix}\).
We denote this row by \(L(E)\), meaning that \(L\) is applied to each
basis vector separately. Thus
\begin{equation}
  L(E)=FM.
  \label{eq:linear-algebra-i:basis-image-matrix}
\end{equation}
The matrix is unique because each basis-image expansion is unique.
\end{pendingreview}

The images \(L(e_j)\) may be zero or linearly dependent, so they need
not form a basis of \(W\). The matrix records their coordinates in
\(F\) in every case. Their span and dependencies are studied through
image, rank, and kernel in
\autoref{ch:linear-algebra-i:kernel-image-and-rank}.
\autoref{ex:linear-algebra-i:full-rank-dependent-columns} shows how
dependent images can still span the entire codomain.

\begin{pendingreview}
\autoref{eq:linear-algebra-i:basis-image-matrix} records what happens
to the basis vectors. Linearity explains why the same matrix computes
the image of every input. Let
\[
  v=Ea=e_1a^1+\cdots+e_ma^m.
\]
Applying \(L\) and substituting the basis-image expansions gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    L(v)&=L(e_1a^1+\cdots+e_ma^m)\\
      &=L(e_1)a^1+\cdots+L(e_m)a^m\\
      &=(f_1M^1_1+\cdots+f_nM^n_1)a^1\\
      &\quad+\cdots+(f_1M^1_m+\cdots+f_nM^n_m)a^m.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Regrouping by the output basis vectors produces
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    L(v)&=f_1(M^1_1a^1+\cdots+M^1_ma^m)\\
      &\quad+\cdots+f_n(M^n_1a^1+\cdots+M^n_ma^m).
  \end{aligned}
\]
The coefficient of \(f_i\) is the \(i\)-th entry of \(Ma\).
Consequently,
\[
  L(v)=L(Ea)=L(E)a=(FM)a=F(Ma).
\]
The input-coordinate column is \(a\), the output-coordinate column is
\(Ma\), and the output vector itself is \(F(Ma)\). These expressions
distinguish the coordinates from the vectors they represent. The matrix
\(M\) converts the \(E\)-coordinates of the input into the
\(F\)-coordinates of its image. For a zero-dimensional space, the
corresponding basis and coordinate column are empty, and the formulas
use empty sums.
\end{pendingreview}

Both routes through \autoref{fig:linear-algebra-i:linear-map-coordinates}
give the same output vector, since \(L(Ea)=F(Ma)\).
\begin{marginfigure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/linear-map-coordinates}
  \caption[Coordinates of a linear map]{Across the top, \(M\) acts on
    coordinates. Across the bottom, \(L\) acts on vectors. The vertical
    arrows reconstruct vectors in the bases \(E\) and \(F\).}
  \label{fig:linear-algebra-i:linear-map-coordinates}
\end{marginfigure}

In Einstein notation, repeated upper and lower indices are summed over their
ranges. With this convention, the expressions above become
\[
  L(e_j)=f_iM^i_j,\qquad v=e_ja^j,\qquad L(v)=f_iM^i_ja^j.
\]
The matrix depends on the map and on both ordered bases.
The map \(L\) specifies what happens to a vector. Its matrix specifies
how to compute the corresponding output coordinates from the chosen input
coordinates. Changing either basis changes the coordinate descriptions
used in this calculation. The identity \(L(E)=FM\) keeps these roles
separate and makes their connection explicit.

\subsection{Composition}

Suppose \(L_1:V_0\to V_1\) and \(L_2:V_1\to V_2\) have matrices
\(M_1\) and \(M_2\) in compatible ordered bases, so that
\[
  L_1(E_0)=E_1M_1,\qquad L_2(E_1)=E_2M_2.
\]
Then
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    (L_2\circ L_1)(E_0)
      &=L_2(L_1(E_0))\\
      &=L_2(E_1M_1)=L_2(E_1)M_1\\
      &=E_2(M_2M_1).
  \end{aligned}
\]
The matrix of the composite is \(M_2M_1\), in that order.
For \(v=E_0a\), this gives \((L_2\circ L_1)(v)=E_2M_2M_1a\).

% Source: images/primitives/IMG_9447.jpeg, lower half.
\begin{example}
  Rotate the real plane through \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise and then
  apply the shear \(S(x,y)=(x+y,y)\). In standard coordinates, the
  rotation and shear have matrices
  \[
    R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},
    \qquad S=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.
  \]
  The rotation acts first, so the combined action is
  \[
    S(Rv)=(SR)v,
    \qquad
    SR=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}.
  \]
  Indeed, \((x,y)^T\) first becomes \((-y,x)^T\), which the shear
  sends to \((x-y,x)^T\).
  \autoref{fig:linear-algebra-i:rotation-shear-grid} follows the basis
  arrows and the grid through these two stages. The columns of \(SR\)
  record their final images, so the product contains the complete combined
  transformation.
\end{example}

\subsection{Invertible maps and inverse matrices}

\begin{definition}
  A linear map \(L:V\to W\) is invertible if every \(w\in W\) is the
  image of exactly one \(v\in V\). Equivalently, it is both injective
  and surjective. Its inverse \(L^{-1}:W\to V\) satisfies
  \[
    L^{-1}\circ L=I_V,\qquad L\circ L^{-1}=I_W.
  \]
\end{definition}

% Lucas's November 10, 2026 theorem-proof request authorizes this
% prerequisite argument for inverse matrices and determinant multiplicativity.
\begin{pendingreview}
\begin{proposition}[The inverse of a linear map]
  \label{thm:linear-algebra-i:inverse-linearity}
  The inverse of an invertible linear map \(L:V\to W\) is linear.
  If \(E\) is a basis of \(V\), then \(L(E)\) is a basis of \(W\).
  In particular, finite-dimensional \(V\) and \(W\) have equal dimension.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Let \(w_1,w_2\in W\) and \(a,b\in\mathbb F\). Put
  \(v_1=L^{-1}(w_1)\) and \(v_2=L^{-1}(w_2)\). Linearity gives
  \[
    L(av_1+bv_2)=aL(v_1)+bL(v_2)=aw_1+bw_2.
  \]
  Applying \(L^{-1}\) yields
  \[
    L^{-1}(aw_1+bw_2)=aL^{-1}(w_1)+bL^{-1}(w_2),
  \]
  proving that the inverse is linear.

  To prove the basis assertion, every \(w\in W\) is \(L(v)\) for
  some \(v\in V\). Expanding \(v\) in \(E\) and applying \(L\)
  expresses \(w\) as a finite linear combination of the vectors in
  \(L(E)\). Thus \(L(E)\) spans \(W\).
  If distinct \(e_1,\ldots,e_q\in E\) satisfy
  \(\sum_{j=1}^q a_jL(e_j)=0\), then
  \(L(\sum_{j=1}^q a_je_j)=0=L(0)\). Injectivity gives
  \(\sum_{j=1}^q a_je_j=0\), and independence of \(E\) forces
  every \(a_j=0\). The images are independent and form a basis.
  Since injectivity also keeps distinct basis vectors distinct,
  the two bases have the same size, giving the dimension assertion.
\end{proof}
\end{pendingreview}

Let \(E\) and \(F\) be bases of \(V\) and \(W\), and write
\[
  L(E)=FM,\qquad L^{-1}(F)=EN.
\]
Using \autoref{thm:linear-algebra-i:inverse-linearity} gives
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    F&=L(L^{-1}(F))=L(EN)=L(E)N=FMN,\\
    E&=L^{-1}(L(E))=L^{-1}(FM)=L^{-1}(F)M=ENM.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Since \(E\) and \(F\) are bases, \(MN=NM=I\), hence \(N=M^{-1}\).
The inverse map is represented by the inverse matrix.

\subsection{Changing both bases}

Let \(E'=EA\) and \(F'=FB\) be new ordered bases, with invertible
change-of-basis matrices \(A\) and \(B\). If \(L(E)=FM\), then
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    L(E')&=L(EA)=L(E)A\\
      &=FMA=F'B^{-1}MA.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Therefore, the matrix \(M'\) satisfying \(L(E')=F'M'\) is
\begin{equation}
  M'=B^{-1}MA.
  \label{eq:linear-algebra-i:change-of-basis-for-maps}
\end{equation}
